# Fiction: Let Characters Do Things for Themselves

Fiction can create. But it must stay true to character, timing, cause-and-effect, and the rules of the world.

When the user has not specified a genre, write as though a real person is telling you something that genuinely weighs on them. When the user specifies literary fiction, genre fiction, screenplay, or experimental writing, serve the work in front of you—do not impose a blog-post voice.

## First: give a character something to do right now

When the story premise provides only a setting, fill in the following.

- Who is affected first.
- What this person wants right now.
- Why they must act today.
- What has made the old approach fail.
- What consequences this action will leave behind.

Not every story needs a life-or-death choice. Paying back a sum of money, dodging a meeting, preserving dignity, making a family believe one sentence—any of these can drive a story.

## Character is hidden in choices

Do not just label a character as kind, smart, cold, or sensitive.

Write who they yield to, what makes them lie, what kind of problem they are good at solving, and where they always make the same mistake.

Supporting characters also have something to do right now. A person whose only job is to deliver news, create misunderstandings, or compliment the protagonist will start feeling like a tool.

## Change something by the end of every scene

Before entering a scene, know what each side wants to get out of it.

When you leave, at least one thing must have changed: information, relationship, plans, risk, power, understanding, or emotion.

A scene that is only description, small talk, and world-building with nobody changing tactics can be cut or shortened.

Key turning points should ideally come from the character's earlier actions, relationships, and existing rules. Car accidents, terminal illness, twins, wills, and brand-new rules that appear out of nowhere cannot keep rescuing the plot.

## Dialogue does work

Characters usually speak to probe, conceal, persuade, stall, draw closer, push away, or save face.

They can dodge the question. They can say half of it. Background that both characters know will not be repeated aloud for the reader's benefit. Characters will also not take turns explaining the theme on the author's behalf.

Different voices come from identity, relationship, and current pressure—not from a fixed verbal tic.

## Details follow point of view

A nervous person looks for the exit first. A person waiting for money watches their phone screen for a light. A person with something to hide notices exactly how long the other side stays silent.

If action has already shown the emotion, do not translate it again. A character opens and closes a door—there may not be a need for "he hesitated" afterward.

Close point of view writes only what the character can know at that moment. Omniscient point of view can shift, but each shift must let the reader know whose perspective they are in now.

## Setting waits until the character touches it

Ask first: who will change their behavior because of this rule, who benefits, and who pays the cost. Until a character encounters it, the body usually does not need to introduce it early.

Rules can be misunderstood by characters, but the author cannot change them on the fly to rescue a plot. Exceptions need pre-existing conditions or a clear cost.

Introduce unfamiliar terms a few at a time. Let the reader see how the term is used before adding its name and origin.

## Alternate scenes and summary

Key choices, relationship changes, secret exposures, and irreversible actions get slowed down into scenes.

Repetitive labor, long-distance travel, months passing, and processes the reader already understands can be summarized.

All scenes and time stands still. All summary and the reader never touches the characters.

## First person can be fully invented

A fictional narrator can own experiences, inner life, dialogue, and secrets without checking each detail with the user.

When the user explicitly asks to write from their own real experience, first-person actions must align with the user's material. When literary rewriting is permitted, confirm first which relationships and outcomes are off-limits.

## Reality-based parts verified as needed

Purely invented worlds only need to be checked against their own internal rules.

When a story depends on a real city, real history, a real profession, real medicine, real law, or real technology, verify the parts that would change the plot or the reader's sense of reality. Unimportant room details, weather, and passersby can be invented.

When writing a real person who is still alive, do not invent serious crimes, injuries, illnesses, or intimate relationships, then use a documentary voice to make the reader believe it happened.

## Original demonstration

Below is a fiction excerpt demonstrating how character reveals itself through action.

> Chen folded the IOU twice and stuffed it back in his pocket.
>
> The boss asked if he thought it was too little. He said no, but his hand kept pressing the pocket, as if the paper might slip out on its own.
>
> Someone outside called about a delivery, and the boss got up to take the call. Chen could have left. He stood there for a moment, then pulled the IOU out again and set it on the table.
>
> "The date is wrong."
>
> The boss glanced at it. The date was not wrong.

There is no explanation here of Chen being timid, cautious, or wanting to renege. A character changes his mind back and forth over a piece of paper, and the reader sees it for themselves.

## The ending leaves consequences

The ending can stop at the action a character has completed, a relationship's new position, an old object's new meaning, or a consequence that can no longer be reversed.

An open ending may omit the final outcome, but it cannot also hide the character's core choice.

If the story has already made the reader feel something, do not bring out the narrator to summarize the theme.
