# Revise: Save the Living Parts First, Then Clean the Sentences

This file is read only after the first draft is complete. It must not participate in first-draft generation.

The goal of revision is not to make the piece airtight. First protect what is already alive, then dismantle the poses the AI put on.

The seven passes below default to modern prose. Poetry, scripts, character dialogue, and experimental writing only execute the fact/setting boundary checks, the items the user explicitly requested, and the hard prohibitions; all other checks defer to the specific genre. Do not damage an originally successful poem by forcing its skeleton to sound like an essay, or a dialogue by making it read like an explanatory article.

## First pass: who is speaking

After reading, answer four questions.

- Nonfiction: what gives the author the right to know this. Fiction: what the narrator could know at this moment.
- Where can you tell the author genuinely cares.
- Which judgments are supported by material, and which emotions are supported by action or relationship.
- Which paragraphs could have been written identically by a different AI.

The fourth category gets priority. For nonfiction, add source trails, author choices, and specific material. For fiction, add narrative position, character actions, and scene changes. If there is nothing to add, delete.

Do not fix the anonymity problem by adding "I think," "honestly," or "consider this." Voice comes from choices, not pronouns.

## Second pass: how the article moves forward

Label each paragraph with one primary function: action, fact, explanation, example, question, judgment, background, or emotion.

Several consecutive paragraphs doing the same thing will usually drag. A paragraph that does not pick up the question, action, or result left by the previous one indicates the order needs adjustment.

Ask what the new paragraph adds. If it merely restates the previous paragraph in different words, merge or delete. The most common form of padding in AI text is explaining one idea four times.

Nonfiction: write a non-public material source for each paragraph—it may be user material, a public source, or a verifiable process. Fiction: mark which character's goal, action, discovery, or relationship change supports this paragraph; do not fabricate sources for created details. If you can only write "this is further explanation" or "this is a possible implication," the paragraph has no new material. Trim until the piece's length matches its material; do not keep paragraphs to hit a word count.

When you encounter history, technology, institutions, or data, ask which question it is answering. If removing it leaves the character's choices, the stakes, and the judgment unchanged, the knowledge arrived too early or was never needed.

Run a compression test. If deleting a third of the piece leaves the facts, actions, judgments, and reading experience nearly unchanged, the original was padded. Keep the shortened version.

For a character piece, watch where he is, what he does, what constraints he hits, and what happens after. For fiction, watch who wants what, and what has changed by the end of the scene.

When the entire piece advances through "there's another layer" and "the deeper level is," delete these signposts. Let factual differences and the reader's questions do the connecting.

## Third pass: strip performative prose

### False profundity

Circle every sentence that looks great as a standalone screenshot but adds no fact, explanation, or emotion when returned to the body. Delete most of them.

When several consecutive paragraphs all end with a short judgment, keep the one with the most material behind it. Ordinary paragraphs end with ordinary sentences.

### False specificity

Search for exact times, weather, facial expressions, room furnishings, cigarettes and alcohol, food, and sudden direct quotes. Nonfiction: ask each where it comes from and whether it changes what follows. Fiction: ask only whether it belongs to the current point of view and whether it affects action, relationship, or atmosphere.

Nonfiction with no source and no function: delete. Fiction with no function: delete. Do not let decorative details masquerade as firsthand experience, and do not let fictional details become uniformly placed props.

### False colloquialism

Terms like "dude," "you guys," "let me tell you," "grab some coffee and settle in" stay only when the speaker or character would genuinely use them.

Do not add profanity, misspellings, trailing ellipses, or parenthetical asides to manufacture a human feel. When the character's identity, experimental writing, or source material genuinely calls for them, treat them as part of the work.

### Abstract nouns

When abstract nouns appear consecutively, ask who did what, affected whom, and where the money, time, and responsibility fall.

A sentence that could be swapped into ten different business articles proves it has no material belonging to this piece.

### Metaphor collisions

Nonfiction: first write the borrowed domain on the side. Roads, warfare, architecture, temperature, warehousing, ocean, body.

When three distinct metaphor systems appear in close proximity, revert all of them to their literal meanings. If the literal meaning is already clear, none need to go back in. Fiction, poetry, and lyrical writing: check whether the images belong to the same sensory and narrative world, and do not mechanically delete by count.

Abstract product pieces are especially prone to borrowing warehouses, drawers, temperature, death, collapse, waves, keys, and foundations. When the subject of the writing is none of these things, delete first. If meaning is not lost after deletion, do not replace with a new metaphor.

## Fourth pass: hear whether the English flows

This pass checks modern prose. Poetry line breaks, character dialogue, and stage directions may use fragments, clipped sentences, and incomplete constructions—judge by the applicable format and genre references.

### Lead with the verb

Circle "what makes X is Y," "the more X, the more Y," "the truly X ones are those that Y," "the X who Y," and sentences with multiple "of" or stacked prepositional phrases.

Find the real agent. Put them and the action up front. Reasons, timing, conditions, and examples follow behind.

Do not mechanically chop into short sentences. Consecutive short sentences become drumbeats. Complex relationships may need a long sentence, as long as the core appears early.

Paragraphs also need variation in weight. Ten straight paragraphs of one sentence each—merge the ones that flow naturally together. Several paragraphs of similar length—check whether each was produced from the same mold.

### What did the previous sentence hand to the next

Follow each comma and period forward. The next sentence picks up the person, object, action, or result the previous one just introduced—this usually reads smoothly. If it suddenly shifts subject, relying only on "this," "it," or "they" to connect, the reader may misidentify who is meant.

When reference is clear, delete repeated subjects and mechanical connectors. When two people are both present, the previous sentence contains multiple nouns, or a large block of text intervenes, write the name back in.

Long modifiers: break them apart. Let the person or thing appear first, then supply their background, conditions, and constraints. Three consecutive instances of "of" or a noun phrase where you've forgotten the subject by the time you reach the noun—rewrite.

### Pauses have weight

Read aloud. At a comma you should still be able to keep speaking; at a period one thing should have come to rest. Consecutive short sentences that sound like table-pounding: merge. A long sentence with no place to land: break it up.

A short sentence after a long one can indeed land the point. Ending every paragraph this way becomes a mechanical drumbeat. Let ordinary places end ordinarily.

### One fewer explanation after the action

Circle the emotional explanations that follow actions, objects, direct quotes, and metaphors. If the reader can already see the character hesitating, being afraid, holding back, or pretending to be tough—delete the explanation.

Whitespace omits only what the reader can already reach on their own. Key facts, causation, character choices, and necessary conditions must still be stated clearly.

### Plain language and classical echoes should not fight each other

Check the single-syllable versus longer word pairs: "buy/purchase," "fix/repair," "look/observe," "use/utilize." Choose based on the speaker and occasion. Do not swap everything to formal long words, nor compress everything to monosyllables for archaic effect.

Idioms, allusions, and formal constructions: read each in isolation. If deleting it makes the piece more natural, delete. If it requires a plain-language explanation immediately after, also delete. If it does not match the character's identity, the topic, or the piece's tone, definitely delete.

## Fifth pass: clear prohibitions

Search the entire text for em dashes and en-dash-style dashes; zero in the body. Search for colons; keep only the ones introducing a character's direct speech. All other colons—rewrite. URLs, code, and machine-generated field names may stay.

Clear false opposition by checking action, not just wording. Go paragraph by paragraph: does this first erect a misunderstanding the reader never had, then knock it down to inflate the author's insight? Below are the common disguises of this same move—if any match, rewrite as a direct statement.

| Disguise | Example |
| --- | --- |
| Literal opposition | It's not A, it's B. Not A but rather B. It's not about A but about B |
| Shortened form | Not A—B. Not A; B is the real answer |
| Split-sentence form | It seems like A. But actually B. On the surface A. In reality B |
| Word-swap form | Rather than A, one might say B. Instead of A, more accurately B |
| Assumption form | You think A, but actually B. I always thought A, until I realized B |
| Concession form | It appears A, but is in fact B. Everyone says A, but the truth is B. The answer is the exact opposite |
| Inflation form | A doesn't matter; B is what matters. The truly valuable thing has nothing to do with A |

There is only one way to fix it: find the judgment this sentence originally intended to make, state it directly, and place the supporting evidence beside it. When the piece has genuinely walked the reader through a process from misunderstanding to correction using real material, one self-correction may stay—but it must not wear any of the disguises above.

Search for three or more parallel structures with the same pattern. The same lead word or sentence pattern repeated three times in a row: keep two, rework or delete the third.

Search for lyrical sentences that pair abstract nouns with concrete verbs. "Time keeps," "years polish," "anxiety takes shape," "loneliness germinates"—restore to who did what and what happened.

Search for nominalizations. "Undertook," "implemented," "completed," "served the function of," "held the significance of"—restore to direct verbs.

Search for "plainly speaking," "to put it bluntly," "to get straight to the point." Delete all or rewrite.

Check for AI-favorite insight signposts. "What's more subtle is," "there's another layer," "only half right," "notably," "it should be pointed out," "in a sense"—when used to elevate a paragraph, delete. When the text genuinely discusses floor levels, quantities, or the literal meaning of a quote, keep.

The following words are treated as absolute prohibitions in this skill. Empowerment, lever, closed business loop, value loop, capability repository, align end-to-end, underlying logic, top-level design, cognitive leap, value release, capability building, cost reduction and efficiency, content matrix, full chain, combo play, unlock imagination, structural opportunity, key proposition, deep logic, technology foundation, public foundation, technology sovereignty, single point of failure, main spine, material anchor, cognitive increment, iterative loop. Rewrite using people, actions, money, time, and consequences.

The following words receive context-only checks. Sediment, granularity, align, collaborate, pipeline, ecological niche, mindshare, paradigm, methodology, core variable, playbook, imagination, closed loop, not losing face. Keep when they genuinely mean chemical precipitation, layout alignment, game tactics, room for imagination in real estate or art criticism, control loops, or "not embarrassing." Rewrite when they merely elevate ordinary things.

Another batch of AI-favorite lyrical words to review. Arrive, anchor, glimmer, fold, fullness, searing, weightless, naked, peel, sharp, hard, soft. All perfectly good words when describing concrete things; delete when used to dress up abstract concepts. Same for bracketed self-coined phrases—more than three in a piece and they are likely manufacturing catchphrases; dismantle them.

Check that hard-prohibition scripts match this section exactly. When adding or removing hard prohibitions, update the script in the same revision pass; do not let the script develop its own parallel ruleset.

When a direct quote hits a prohibition, rewrite as indirect speech. Prohibitions must reach zero before the piece can be submitted.

## Sixth pass: verify reality or settings

Nonfiction: verify times, numbers, identities, quotations, causation, and first-person actions. Verification notes stay behind the scenes.

Fiction: verify what characters know, time and space, injuries and objects, and world rules. When emotion is already supported by action, delete the explanation that follows immediately.

Mixed-genre: verify that the parts requiring factual accuracy and the creative space are not impersonating each other.

## Seventh pass: check the ending

Delete each of the last two paragraphs individually and re-read. If the piece is stronger without one, let the article end earlier.

The final paragraph recapitulates the entire piece—even if beautifully written, delete first. Content already made clear earlier in the piece does not need to be proved again.

Search for "era, civilization, future, world, history, miracle, everyone, all of humanity." If the body has not been working at this scale throughout, bring the ending back to concrete facts, character actions, or the current judgment. When the user-supplied title inherently contains such large words, the title stays as given; this rule governs body text and ending only.

When the opening object reappears at the end, confirm the reader's understanding of it has changed. If meaning is unchanged, there is no need to check back in.

## Use the check script

After saving a long piece as text or Markdown, run:

```text
python3 scripts/check_prose.py manuscript.md
```

The script flags colons, dashes, false opposition, hard-stop words, AI signposts, and absolutely prohibited jargon as failures. Words requiring contextual judgment are listed as warnings. It also warns about consecutive short sentences, overuniform paragraphs, formulaic openings, and multiple metaphor systems.

The script only detects patterns; it cannot judge whether the piece has a human voice. Warning items require a manual check against the material and the speaking position. Poetry, scripts, and experimental writing treat only the script's failure items as hard requirements; prose pattern warnings defer to the specific genre.

## Final cold read

Temporarily forget all the rules and answer only these questions:

- Where do I believe the author actually knows this material?
- Where am I willing to keep reading?
- Which paragraph reads like a person working through a problem, and which reads like an AI completing a writing task?
- Which sentence claims more than its material supports?
- At which sentence has the piece already ended?
