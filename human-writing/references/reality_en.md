# Reality-Based Writing: An Extra Layer of Fact-Checking

This document covers real people, history, news, products, industries, data, tutorials, reviews, commercial copy, and first-person accounts.

Fact-checking stays behind the scenes. Its job is to make the piece hold up, not to take over the voice.

## First: sort materials by reliability

Internally, classify important materials into five tiers.

1. Facts directly provable by reliable sources.
2. Statements from the company, institution, or people involved.
3. Recollection from third parties and media summaries.
4. The author's inferences drawn from materials.
5. Things that remain unknown.

Record the date, source, and framing. Positions, policies, prices, product capabilities, licenses, and software versions change—reconfirm before writing.

Prioritize primary sources: official documents, papers, regulatory filings, and complete interviews with the people involved. Media is useful for background and cross-referencing. When sources conflict, you cannot pick whichever one best serves the narrative and present it as settled.

## The body only carries attributions readers actually need

State confirmed facts directly. Company statements, third-party recollection, and contested claims should identify who said what the first time they appear.

Unknowns that would change the reader's assessment of a person, the article's conclusion, or the reader's next step get stated once in the body. Everything else is cut or moved to a sources note.

Phrases like "public records show," "it has not been confirmed," or "what can be confirmed is" appearing in a row signal that fact-checking notes leaked into the body text. Pull them back behind the scenes and reorganize.

If the entire article relies on source attribution and "cannot be confirmed" to fill space, the material is insufficient. State the biggest gap once near the top, put detailed citations at the end, and let the body continue with what has been established.

When the article depends entirely on public materials, leave one genuine trace of research near the beginning. For example: "I cross-checked every résumé and interview I could find, and the material from these three years is genuinely sparse." This shows why the author is entitled to keep going. You do not need to re-prove the author's thoroughness every two paragraphs.

## User experiences cannot be ghostwritten

If the user said "saw," you cannot expand it to "tried." If the user provided a screenshot, you cannot fill in the registration, payment, and successful run. If the user does not remember the exact words, write the gist or ask them to confirm.

When personal material is genuinely needed, ask at most three questions. What did they do, what surprised them, and how they understand it now. If the user asks you to write first, use whatever information is available and keep the gaps honest.

When the user gives permission to fictionalize, you may restructure and create. The delivery format should make clear that it has been fictionalized.

## Find real pressure before writing a person

Map the timeline first, then check cause-and-effect and motivation.

Only write a choice when the material can prove the person made it. If the material only shows they left one company and moved to another city, write the action, the conditions at the time, and the outcome that followed.

Later success does not retroactively prove that every earlier step was visionary. Graduating, moving, and changing jobs do not automatically become turning points.

Tension in a profile can come from these places.

- Income suddenly cutting off.
- A piece of technology that never works.
- A partnership changing.
- Market rules and tools shifting.
- Not enough money, time, or people.
- A documented decision with real consequences.

If the source has no weather, body language, room details, dialogue, or inner thought, do not invent them. When material is sparse, add verified industry and period context, or shorten the piece.

Context must also change the person's conditions. Adding only period atmosphere, jargon explanations, and hindsight is still padding.

## Historical narrative: keep three things separate

Chronology says two things happened in sequence.

Causation says the first caused the second.

Motivation says a person acted because of a particular idea.

If the source only supports sequence, you cannot smuggle in causation with "therefore," "as a result," or "this made them decide." If the source has no first-person account, you cannot write their inner monologue.

The pressure in historical stories is often already in the material. Geography, troop strength, institutions, available paths, and the consequences of failure, laid out clearly, will let the person emerge naturally.

## News and products: confirm where things stand

Separate what is already available, what is in invite-only testing, what has been announced but not yet delivered, what is a long-term vision, and what is industry speculation.

The headline goes only as far as current facts allow. The body can acknowledge "not yet available," but that cannot rescue a headline that overpromised.

Official materials answer "how the publisher wants it understood." The author also needs to answer "what an ordinary reader will actually encounter." You can add limitations, costs, real workflows, affected parties, or questions the publisher has not answered.

When there is no new material, deliver an accurate explainer. Do not dress it up as exclusive insight.

## Industry impact lands on specific roles

Spell out who buys, who deploys, who pays, who takes on extra work, who may lose an order, who is responsible when something breaks, and where the old way hurts most.

Stop listing companies once you have proved the pattern. Pick two or three examples with different roles to show the relationships, and mention the rest in a sentence.

Before claiming the market is huge, find the earliest willing buyer, their current pain, and the first constraint they face.

## After writing data, take one more step

Verify time period, units, sample, methodology, and what is being compared. Percentage and percentage point are not the same.

After a number, explain what it means: who spends how much more time, saves how much money, gains what option, or faces what limitation. A single case illustrates one corner—it does not automatically represent the entire industry.

## Judgment needs boundaries

State the opposing side's strongest argument accurately before saying which interpretation you currently find more convincing under the available material.

A boundary stated once is enough. Adding "possibly," "perhaps," and "cannot rule out" to every sentence makes the author disappear from the piece.

Recommendations should carry cost and conditions for use. Business, medical, legal, and financial content must distinguish general information, evidence-based conclusions, and advice tailored to an individual.

## How to deliver sources

If the user only wants the article, keep the body smooth and list key sources at the end as needed. When the original source title contains punctuation that breaks formatting, replace the link text with an accurate short description while keeping the URL as-is.

If the user requests item-by-item verification, provide a fact table or annotations. Links prove facts; they cannot replace the author's explanation.
