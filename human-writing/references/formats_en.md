# How Different Formats Change the Clothes

This document only handles format differences. The format the user specifies overrides the default long-post approach; speaker, material, progression, and factual boundaries are still determined by the main workflow. Poetry, screenplays, dialogue-heavy work, and experimental writing do not get forced into the full body-and-paragraph shape of modern prose.

## Q&A answers (Quora, Zhihu, Reddit)

Address the question first. If the reader asks "why," give an explanation as early as possible. If the reader asks "what is it like," the body should have experience, observation, or a clear statement of where the knowledge comes from.

It can be short or long. Do not start by thanking the asker, do not preview "I will answer in three points," and do not reshape the answer into a blog post and paste it back in.

Professional questions should be explained until the reader can act on them. Personal questions should clarify who the experience applies to—do not turn one experience into a universal law.

## Forum long posts

Explain why this thread exists, then follow the timeline, investigation, or firsthand process. Asides, additions, corrections, and intermediate pauses are allowed.

In a serial, each update must bring a new fact, new progress, or new understanding. Do not manufacture fake engagement with "if people are reading, I will keep going."

Real experiences preserve what was not known at the time. Today's understanding cannot be retroactively projected as something you saw through from the start.

## Newsletter and blog long reads

The body can be tighter than a forum post, but the speaker must still be present. Pick the headline last, from where the article has already landed honestly.

Long reads are held up by material, explanation, and process. If the most recent thousand words only make the same point louder, cut them or keep researching.

Subheadings are for navigation, not for writing a conclusion for every section in advance. Sections do not need to be equal length.

## Profile and historical long reads

Check against the timeline, then write in whatever order the reader needs to understand it.

A stretch of biography only gets expanded when it changes the conditions that follow. If you have covered school, job title, and city and the character still has not done anything, the piece reads like a résumé.

Tension comes from documented change. Not every graduation, move, and job switch should be written as a fateful bet.

## News, product, and industry explainers

Write what is new first, then explain why an ordinary person should care.

Press materials answer what the publisher wants to say. The author must add usage, deployment, pricing, data, liability, switching costs, and unanswered questions.

Stop listing names once you have proved the trend. Pick two or three roles at different positions to show the impact.

## Opinions and commentary

Ground the debate in specific facts, causal chains, or value choices. Handle the strongest counterargument accurately before giving the judgment the author currently holds.

When there is no personal experience, do not fabricate "I saw it with my own eyes." How the author selects material, explains causation, and which statements they are willing to stand behind already forms a voice.

## Personal narrative

Find the clearest action, object, scene, and one surprise. Preserve what you understood then and also the change you see looking back today.

When an object appears at the beginning and end, both its real-world function and its later meaning must hold up. Do not invent a cup, a bus ticket, or an old letter just to create a callback.

## Short-form and social content

Keep one job only. Deliver a piece of news, share one observation, give one judgment, leave one detail, or let a character complete a small change.

The first sentence should reach the subject, action, or outcome as fast as possible. Short pieces do not need a forced twist. The last sentence just needs to land the point.

When cutting a long piece down, reselect and reorder—do not just delete your way down the original.

## Tutorials and how-tos

Say early what you will get when finished, what is needed to start, and where you are most likely to get stuck.

Steps follow dependency order. Each step shows the action and the visible result. Explain principles only until the reader can handle common variations.

A tutorial that saves the reader one wrong step has already delivered value. The ending does not need to touch on epiphany, life, or the spirit of the times.

## Reviews and commercial copy

Hands-on material follows real tasks. Why you started using it, what steps you took, where it failed, what the result was, and who it is suited for.

When you only have official material, write an explainer or a testing plan—do not upgrade it to a first-person experience.

Partnership disclosure, public facts, hands-on results, and the author's judgment should all be distinguishable. Writing limitations accurately is often more credible than unrelenting praise.

## Spoken word, podcasts, and talks

Write for the ear. Say a new term in full the first time. Pronouns must have a clear antecedent. Only keep the numbers the listener must remember.

If a sentence cannot be said in one breath, break it apart. Open with orientation and the immediate question, then follow the next question the listener would ask.

Read the whole thing aloud and time it. If it flows on paper but tangles in the mouth, it still needs to change.

## Screenplays and dialogue

Write only what can be seen or heard. Inner life must become behavior, choice, a pause, or words spoken aloud.

Before each scene, figure out what each side wants to get out of it. Lines serve the immediate goal—they do not take turns introducing the setting and the theme.

## Poetry and lyrical writing

Let feeling move. What you see at the start, what touches you in the middle, and how the object has changed by the end.

Choose a small number of images that know each other. Line breaks must change the breathing, stress, or meaning—you cannot just chop prose into shorter lines.
